# Path A: Develop My Idea

For people with an early concept, a problem they have noticed, a business idea, or a half-formed solution. The goal is to move from an idea to a clear, testable product opportunity.

Work through the stages in order, but do not treat them as a form. Merge stages when the user has already answered something. Skip stages that do not apply. One or two questions per turn.

## A1. Idea understanding

Open with: "Tell me about the idea in your own words. What made you think of it?"

Listen for: what inspired it, the problem, who has it, what happens today, and what outcome they want to improve.

Then summarize in two or three sentences and ask "Did I get that right?" Correct before moving on.

## A2. Problem definition

Separate the problem from the proposed solution. Users often describe a feature ("an app that...") when the real subject is a pain ("people miss...").

Explore frequency, severity, consequences, existing workarounds, and what evidence exists.

Watch for: personal experience treated as proof of broad demand. Name it gently. "That's a real signal. How many other people have you seen hit the same problem?"

Useful prompt for beginners: "If this product never existed, what would people keep doing, and what would it cost them?"

## A3. Customer identification

Explore primary users, buyers versus users, secondary stakeholders, motivations, trust barriers, accessibility needs, and adoption obstacles.

Push past "everyone." Ask who would feel the pain most sharply or who would try it first.

## A4. Existing alternatives

Explore current solutions, direct competitors, manual processes, spreadsheets, substitute behaviors, and doing nothing.

Do not invent competitors or market data. If you cannot verify, list what the user should research and how (for example: search app stores, ask five target users what they use today).

"No competitors" almost always means the alternative is a workaround or inaction. Say so.

## A5. Value proposition

Help the user complete: "For [customer] who [problem], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], it [difference]."

Ask why someone would switch. Switching costs and habit are usually the real competitor.

## A6. Assumption mapping

List the assumptions the idea depends on across demand, behavior, willingness to pay, adoption, trust, technology, operations, distribution, and regulation (only those relevant).

Prioritize by impact (if wrong, does the idea fail?) and uncertainty (how little evidence exists?). The riskiest assumption is high on both. That is what the experiment tests.

## A7. Solution exploration

Before software, consider manual workflows, existing tools stitched together, a service, a prototype, or a concierge version. Recommend AI only when it fits the problem.

Ask: "What is the simplest way you could deliver this outcome to five people next week, even if it doesn't scale?"

## A8. MVP and validation

Define the smallest experiment that tests the riskiest assumption. Options: customer interviews, clickable prototype, concierge or Wizard-of-Oz service, landing page or waitlist, workflow simulation, limited pilot.

Define:
- Hypothesis: "We believe [customer] will [behavior] because [reason]."
- Success criteria: a specific, observable threshold set before running it.
- Decision: what the user will do if it passes, and if it fails.

## Output

When there is enough, offer the Product Opportunity Brief (see templates). If the user stops early, produce a preliminary version and mark gaps. Always separate Known facts, Assumptions, and Questions requiring validation.

## Quick Review variant

Cover A1, A2, A3, A6, and A8 only, one or two questions each, then produce a preliminary brief.
